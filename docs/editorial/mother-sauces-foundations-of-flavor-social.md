## Core caption

Most cooks meet the mother sauces as a list to memorize: béchamel, velouté, espagnole, sauce tomate, hollandaise.

Five names. Recite them, pass the quiz, move on.

That list is useful. It is also the least interesting part of the story.

The five are a system, not a trivia answer. Three are built on roux — flour cooked in fat, then finished with milk, stock or brown stock. One is a long, patient cooking-down of tomato. And one contains no flour at all: hollandaise is an emulsion, egg yolk and butter held together by technique and temperature.

The system also had an editor. The grandes sauces in Carême's early nineteenth-century classification numbered four, and allemande was one of them. In Escoffier's *Le Guide Culinaire* (1903), the canonical list became five: allemande was demoted to a derivative of velouté, and tomate and hollandaise took their places. Same kitchen logic. Revised paperwork.

Then each mother divides. Béchamel plus cheese becomes mornay. Velouté enriched becomes suprême. Hollandaise with tarragon becomes béarnaise. The "small sauces" are not new recipes to memorize — they are decisions made at the pass.

Short cook? Watch your roux color.  
Delicate sauce? Blond, not brown.  
Emulsion? Temperature is the whole job.

The goal is not to recite five French names.

**Understand what each sauce is doing — thickening, carrying, emulsifying — and you can build the rest without the list.**

## Carousel sequence

1. **Five Sauces, One Family Tree.** Béchamel, velouté, espagnole, tomate, hollandaise — the mother sauces are a system for building hundreds of other sauces, not a list for a quiz.
2. **Béchamel: Milk Meets White Roux.** The roux cooks just long enough to lose the raw flour taste, then milk goes in. Gentle, pale, and the base for mornay the moment cheese enters the pot.
3. **Velouté: Stock Meets Blond Roux.** Same technique, different liquid and a little more color. Enrich it and you are in suprême territory — which is exactly where allemande ended up.
4. **Espagnole: Brown Stock, Brown Roux, Patience.** The deepest of the roux sauces, built for reduction. It is less a finished sauce than a foundation other sauces get built on.
5. **Sauce Tomate: The Slow One.** No roux required. Tomato, aromatics and time, cooked until it stops tasting raw and starts tasting like a sauce.
6. **Hollandaise: The Emulsion.** Egg yolk and butter, no flour anywhere. Temperature and whisking hold it together — and temperature is what breaks it.
7. **Allemande: The One That Got Demoted.** It held a top spot in Carême's four grandes sauces. Escoffier filed it as a derivative of velouté, and culinary schools have taught his list ever since.
8. **Don't Memorize the Mothers.** Understand what each one is doing — thickening, carrying, emulsifying — then make it work for the dish in front of you.
