## 2. Section-by-section outline with draft copy

### 2.1 Kicker (draft copy)

> Every culinary student learns the same list: béchamel, velouté, espagnole, sauce tomate, hollandaise. Five "mother sauces." Recite them in the right order and you pass.
>
> That list is useful. It is also the least interesting part of the story.
>
> The mother sauces are not really five recipes. They are a way of controlling **thickness, richness, acidity and temperature** — a small set of bases that professional kitchens divide, enrich and re-season into hundreds of working sauces. The list came later; the system is older, more practical, and it was edited at least once by the most famous cookbook in French cooking.

### 2.2 Chef Gringo source rule (draft copy, mirrors the flagship's callout)

> **Chef Gringo source rule:** The mother-sauce classification is a documented convention from named cookbooks, not a discovered law of cooking. Carême's system and Escoffier's system differ, and both are cited here from the works themselves or reputable reference editions. Origin stories attached to individual sauces — including the popular claim that hollandaise is simply a Dutch sauce imported to France — are tradition, not established fact, and are labeled that way wherever they appear.

### 2.3 "Carême had four" — the first system

Key content:
- Early nineteenth-century French professional cooking organized its great sauces into a small family of bases from which others were built. The classification is associated with Marie-Antoine Carême and his multi-volume work on French cooking. **[VERIFY: exact work and dates — *L'Art de la cuisine française au XIXe siècle* (1833–1835, completed posthumously in part) — and that Carême's own framing is "grandes sauces" / "sauces mères" in his text or in later reference summaries of it.]**
- The four: **espagnole, velouté, allemande, béchamel**. **[VERIFY against Carême text or Larousse Gastronomique's summary of Carême's system.]**
- Why a system at all: a professional kitchen needed bases that could be held, divided and finished to order — an operations answer before it was a curriculum answer. (Frame as professional context, not a sourced quote.)
- Draft line: *"Carême was not inventing sauces. He was filing them — and a good filing system is a kitchen tool."*

### 2.4 "Escoffier made it five" — the edit that stuck

Key content:
- In *Le Guide Culinaire* (1903), Auguste Escoffier's classification — the one culinary schools still teach — lists five: **béchamel, velouté, espagnole, sauce tomate, hollandaise**. **[VERIFY the five list against the 1903 text or its standard English translation.]**
- **Allemande's demotion:** Escoffier treats allemande as a derivative of velouté (velouté finished with egg yolk and cream liaison) rather than a mother in its own right. **[VERIFY allemande's placement and description in *Le Guide Culinaire*.]**
- Tomate and hollandaise enter the canonical list. Note the structural point: hollandaise is an emulsion, not a roux sauce — Escoffier's list groups by kitchen function, not by one technique.
- Draft line: *"Same kitchen logic. Revised paperwork. Culinary school has been teaching the revision for over a century."* **[VERIFY "over a century" is arithmetic, not a claim: 1903 → present. Fine as written.]**

### 2.5 A short timeline of how five became the list

Mirror the flagship's timeline section. Entries (all dates **[VERIFY]** before publication):
- Early 1800s — Carême's classification of four grandes sauces.
- 1903 — *Le Guide Culinaire* publishes Escoffier's five.
- Twentieth century — the five-sauce list becomes standard culinary-school curriculum internationally. **[VERIFY / soften: support with CIA *The Professional Chef* or Larousse rather than asserting.]**
- Today — most working kitchens lean hardest on derivatives (demi-glace directions, mornay, béarnais) and treat espagnole as a foundation step rather than a finished sauce. (Label as professional observation/convention, not fact.)

### 2.6 The science of roux

Key content:
- What a roux is: flour cooked in fat, used as a thickener. Cooking the flour first removes raw flavor and coats starch granules in fat, which is the practical reason roux-based sauces resist lumps when liquid is added correctly. **[VERIFY mechanism wording against McGee, *On Food and Cooking*, starch/thickening sections.]**
- Gelatinization: starch granules absorb liquid and swell as the sauce heats, producing thickness; the sauce must actually reach a simmer for the roux to do its full work and lose its pasty edge. **[VERIFY temperature/behavior claims against McGee.]**
- Color vs power: the longer a roux cooks, the deeper its flavor and color — and the **less** thickening power it retains, because prolonged heat breaks the starch down. This is why a brown roux sauce needs more roux (or more reduction) than a white one. **[VERIFY the starch-breakdown explanation against McGee; the trade-off itself is standard professional convention per CIA *The Professional Chef*.]**
- Working ratios are a **modern professional convention**, stated as such (compare the flagship's treatment of 2:1:1): give the conventional roux-to-liquid guidance used in culinary schools and label it convention, not history. **[VERIFY exact ratios against CIA *The Professional Chef* before printing numbers.]**
- Draft line: *"A roux's color is its flavor and its weakness. Choose the color for the dish, then respect what that choice costs you in thickness."*

### 2.7 The science of emulsion — why hollandaise is different

Key content:
- Hollandaise contains no flour: it is an emulsion of butter held in a water-based phase (egg yolk, water/lemon) stabilized by yolk components — lecithin is the emulsifier usually credited. **[VERIFY against McGee's emulsion/egg sections.]**
- Temperature is the control variable: too cool and the butter congeals; too hot and the yolk proteins set and the sauce splits. **[VERIFY any specific temperature figures against a cited source before printing them; otherwise describe the window qualitatively.]**
- Why it breaks: heat, speed (butter added faster than the emulsion can take it), and ratio. Each maps to a fix in the troubleshooting table (§2.10).
- Safety framing per source standards: egg-based sauces carry handling, holding and discard considerations — state limits plainly and point to institutional guidance rather than improvising numbers. **[VERIFY holding guidance against FDA Food Code or equivalent institutional source.]**

### 2.8–2.12 The five mothers — master sections

Structure per sauce (mirrors the flagship's per-component sections): what it is → the professional method → the realistic home method → derivatives that make it useful → one field rule. Draft anchors below; all method detail is written originally for Chef Gringo and must pass the same verification pass as the history.

**2.8 Béchamel.** White roux + milk. The gentlest mother; seasoning restraint is the technique (salt, white pepper, nutmeg as convention — label as convention). Derivatives: **mornay** (cheese), cream sauce directions, mustard and herb finishes used in the exercise (§2.11). Field rule draft: *"If your béchamel tastes like flour, it hasn't finished cooking — not seasoning. Time first, salt second."*

**2.9 Velouté.** Blond roux + white stock. The quiet carrier: flavor comes from the stock, so the stock is the recipe. Derivatives: **suprême** (cream-enriched), **allemande** — placed here deliberately, with a cross-reference to §2.4: *"This is where Escoffier filed it. Argue with him."*

**2.10 Espagnole.** Brown roux + brown stock, classically built with mirepoix and tomato and reduced. Treated honestly as a **foundation step**: its most important descendant is demi-glace (espagnole combined with brown stock and reduced). **[VERIFY demi-glace composition against *Le Guide Culinaire*.]** Home method: a practical, smaller-scale version without pretending a home cook needs a stockpot program. Field rule draft: *"Espagnole is a means, not an end. Judge it by what it becomes."*

**2.11 Sauce Tomate.** Tomato cooked down with aromatics until raw acidity resolves into sauce; classically includes stock and pork elements in Escoffier's version. **[VERIFY Escoffier's sauce tomate composition in *Le Guide Culinaire*; present the modern simpler tomato sauce as the modern convention, clearly separated.]** Derivatives are looser here — say so rather than inventing a family tree.

**2.12 Hollandaise.** The emulsion mother (§2.7 applied). Derivatives: **béarnaise** (tarragon/shallot reduction), **choron** (béarnaise + tomato). **[VERIFY derivative compositions against *Le Guide Culinaire* or Larousse.]** Field rule draft: *"Hollandaise is a temperature you hold, not a sauce you finish. Set the station up before the yolks go on the heat."*

### 2.13 Troubleshooting table (draft contents)

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lumpy béchamel | Liquid added too fast, or roux and liquid at clashing temperatures with no whisking | Whisk hard off heat; strain if needed — no shame in the strainer | Add liquid gradually at the start; whisk continuously until it simmers |
| Broken hollandaise | Overheated yolk, or butter added faster than the emulsion accepts | Whisk in a spoon of hot water; if that fails, start a fresh yolk and whisk the broken sauce into it | Low, indirect heat; butter in a slow stream; hold warm, never hot |
| Skin on a held sauce | Surface evaporation during holding | Whisk it back in for short holds; cover the surface for longer ones | Cover with a lid or cartouche; hold at proper temperature and stir occasionally |
| Raw-flour taste | Roux undercooked or sauce never reached a simmer | Simmer gently a few minutes more and re-taste | Cook the roux to the intended stage and bring the finished sauce to a full simmer |
| Greasy velouté | Fat not fully incorporated, or an over-rich stock | Skim; whisk in a splash of hot stock to re-emulsify | Skim stock first; keep the sauce at a bare simmer, not a rolling boil |

Each row is standard professional practice; the table as a whole still gets a source pass against CIA *The Professional Chef* troubleshooting guidance before publication. **[VERIFY table.]**

### 2.14 The exercise — "Taste the variables" (draft copy, mirrors the flagship's tasting exercise)

> **Want to understand derivatives instead of memorizing them? Make one béchamel and finish it three ways.**
>
> One batch of béchamel, divided into three warm pans. Pan one: plain, seasoned only. Pan two: a handful of grated Gruyère off the heat — you have made mornay. Pan three: a spoon of Dijon and chopped herbs. Taste them in order and notice what actually changed: not the sauce's structure — its *assignment*. Same mother, three different jobs.
>
> **Chef Gringo field rule:** A derivative is a decision, not a recipe. When you can say what the addition is *for*, you are using the system the way it was designed.

(Gruyère as the classic mornay cheese is convention; label as such in the article. **[VERIFY mornay's standard composition against Larousse / *Le Guide Culinaire*.]**)

### 2.15 The bottom line (draft copy)

> The five mothers were never a memory test. They are a short list of ways to control thickness, richness and temperature — written down by Carême, edited by Escoffier, and still doing quiet work every time a cook turns a base into a sauce with a decision instead of a recipe.
>
> Learn what each one is doing. The small sauces will take care of themselves.
